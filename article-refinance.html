When Should You Refinance Your Mortgage? Complete Guide for 2025

By the MortgageWise Team  ·  15 min read  ·  Updated August 2025

← Back to Calculator

Refinancing your mortgage — replacing your current loan with a new one — can be one of the smartest financial moves you make as a homeowner. Done at the right time and for the right reasons, it can save you hundreds of dollars a month and tens of thousands over the life of your loan.

Done wrong, it can cost you money, extend your debt, or leave you worse off than before. The difference usually comes down to timing, math, and understanding what you're actually trying to accomplish.

This guide covers everything you need to know to make that decision confidently.

What Does Refinancing Actually Mean?

When you refinance, you're essentially paying off your existing mortgage with a brand new loan that has different terms. Your old loan disappears and you start fresh with a new interest rate, new term length, and potentially a new monthly payment.

The new loan goes through the same underwriting process as your original mortgage — income verification, credit check, appraisal, title search — and comes with closing costs, typically $3,000–$7,000 depending on your loan size and location.

That upfront cost is the key variable in almost every refinancing decision. It's not free money. You're spending money now to save money later, so the math has to work in your favor.

The Break-Even Rule — The Most Important Concept in Refinancing

The break-even point is how many months it takes for your monthly savings to cover your closing costs. It's the foundational calculation for any refinancing decision.

Break-Even Formula

Closing Costs ÷ Monthly Savings = Break-Even Months

Example: $5,000 closing costs ÷ $200/month savings = 25 months to break even. If you plan to stay in the home more than 25 months, refinancing makes financial sense. If you might sell or move before then, it doesn't.

6 Clear Signs It's Time to Refinance

📉 1. Interest Rates Have Dropped Significantly Since You Bought

The traditional rule of thumb says refinancing makes sense when you can lower your rate by at least 1%. In reality, it depends on your loan balance and how long you plan to stay. On a $400,000 loan, even a 0.5% rate reduction saves about $115/month — enough to justify refinancing if you plan to stay long enough to break even on the closing costs. On a smaller loan, you need a bigger rate drop to make the math work.

📈 2. Your Credit Score Has Improved Significantly

If your credit score was 650 when you bought but has climbed to 760, you may now qualify for a dramatically lower interest rate even if market rates haven't moved. Lenders price risk based on your credit profile, and a 100-point improvement in your score could shave 0.5–1% off your rate independent of anything happening in the broader market. Check what rate you'd qualify for today — you might be surprised.

🛡️ 3. You Want to Eliminate PMI

If you bought with less than 20% down, you're likely paying PMI — private mortgage insurance that typically runs 0.5–1.5% of your loan annually. On a $300,000 loan that's $1,500–$4,500 per year, or $125–$375 per month, paid entirely for the lender's benefit. If your home has appreciated enough that you now have 20%+ equity, refinancing into a new loan without PMI can make sense even if your interest rate doesn't change much. You're eliminating hundreds per month in insurance you no longer need.

⏱️ 4. You Want to Shorten Your Loan Term

Refinancing from a 30-year to a 15-year mortgage means you pay off your home in half the time and save an enormous amount in interest — but your monthly payment will be higher. This strategy makes sense if your income has grown substantially since you bought, you want to be debt-free by a certain age (retirement, for example), and you can comfortably afford the higher payment without stretching your budget.

🔄 5. You Have an ARM and Rates Are Rising

Adjustable-rate mortgages start with a fixed period — typically 5, 7, or 10 years — and then adjust annually. If your fixed period is ending and interest rates have risen, your payment could jump significantly at the first adjustment. Refinancing to a fixed-rate loan before that reset locks in predictability. Even if the fixed rate is slightly higher than your current ARM rate, the certainty is often worth it.

💰 6. You Need to Access Home Equity

A cash-out refinance lets you borrow more than you currently owe and take the difference in cash. People use this for home improvements, debt consolidation, education costs, or other major expenses. The new loan is larger than your old one, so your monthly payment may increase — but mortgage rates are typically lower than personal loan or credit card rates, making this a relatively affordable way to access capital. Be cautious: you're converting unsecured debt into debt secured by your home.

When NOT to Refinance

Refinancing isn't always the right move, even when rates drop. Here are situations where it usually doesn't make financial sense:

  • You're planning to sell within 1–2 years — you won't have time to break even on closing costs
  • You're close to paying off your loan — in the early years of a mortgage, most of your payment goes to interest. In the later years, most goes to principal. Refinancing restarts the amortization clock, meaning you're paying mostly interest again from day one of the new loan. If you have 8 years left, refinancing to a new 30-year loan dramatically extends your debt.
  • Your credit has worsened — you might not qualify for a rate better than what you have
  • You're extending the term just to lower the payment — reducing your payment by stretching from 20 years remaining to a new 30-year loan will likely cost you significantly more interest over time, even at a lower rate
  • Your home value has dropped significantly — if you're underwater (owe more than the home is worth), refinancing options are limited

The Refinancing Process Step by Step

  1. Check your current loan details — rate, remaining balance, years left, prepayment penalties (rare but check)
  2. Check your credit score — the rate you'll be offered depends heavily on this
  3. Get quotes from at least 3 lenders — rates vary more than people realize, and shopping takes 30 minutes but can save thousands
  4. Calculate your break-even point — make sure you'll stay long enough for the savings to cover closing costs
  5. Lock your rate — once you choose a lender, lock your rate to protect against market movement during the process
  6. Submit your documents — same financial documents as your original mortgage
  7. Appraisal — your lender orders a new appraisal to confirm current home value (typically $300–$600)
  8. Underwriting — lender reviews everything (2–4 weeks)
  9. Close — sign documents and pay closing costs. Your new loan starts.

The whole process typically takes 30–45 days from application to closing.

💡 No-Closing-Cost Refinance: Some lenders offer refinances with no upfront closing costs — they roll the costs into your loan balance or charge a slightly higher rate instead. This can make sense if you're not sure how long you'll stay or if cash is tight. Just understand that "no closing costs" doesn't mean "free" — you're paying in a different way.

Frequently Asked Questions About Refinancing

How many times can you refinance your home?

There's no legal limit on how many times you can refinance. However, each refinance has closing costs and resets your amortization schedule, so doing it too frequently rarely makes financial sense. Most financial advisors suggest waiting at least 12–24 months between refinances and always running the break-even calculation first.

Does refinancing hurt your credit score?

Refinancing involves a hard credit inquiry, which typically drops your score 5–10 points temporarily. Multiple mortgage inquiries within a 45-day window count as just one inquiry for scoring purposes. The impact is usually minor and recovers within a few months — it shouldn't be a reason not to refinance if the math makes sense.

Can I refinance if I have bad credit?

It depends on how bad. FHA streamline refinances have more flexible requirements if you already have an FHA loan. For conventional refinances, most lenders want at least a 620. If your credit has declined significantly since you bought, you may not be able to refinance at all, or the rate offered may not be better than what you have.

When is the best time of year to refinance?

Interest rates fluctuate based on economic conditions, not seasons. There's no reliably "best" time of year. The best time to refinance is when the rate you qualify for today is meaningfully lower than what you have, your break-even timeline works for your plans, and your financial profile (credit, income, equity) is in good shape.

What's a cash-out refinance and is it a good idea?

A cash-out refinance lets you borrow more than you owe and receive the difference in cash. It's useful for home improvements, debt consolidation, or large expenses. It's generally a good idea when mortgage rates are low relative to other borrowing options, the funds will be used for something that adds value, and you're not risking your housing stability for discretionary spending. It's not a good idea if you're using it to fund lifestyle spending or if you're already stretched thin on payments.
